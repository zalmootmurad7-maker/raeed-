## Script to record (voice: Max, Eleven v4)

**Cold open**

Eight types of black holes.
One is smaller than an atom.
One is bigger than our entire solar system.
And one explodes in about one second.

**01 · Micro**

Number one: micro black holes.
Take Mount Everest, all of it,
and crush it until it becomes a black hole.
Its radius: zero point two four picometers.
About four thousand times smaller than a nanometer.
Blow an atom up to the size of a football stadium,
and this black hole is a beach ball on the pitch.
Theory says the smallest possible one is about twenty-two micrograms.
Lighter than a grain of salt.
Physicists have even asked if cosmic rays, or the Large Hadron Collider,
could make one for a split second.
The catch: the small ones explode.
Black holes slowly leak energy, called Hawking radiation.
And the smaller they are, the faster they leak.
Everest's version would outlive the universe.
But shrink it to about two hundred and thirty tons, one blue whale,
and it blows itself apart in about one second.
The tiny ones some feared at the collider? Gone in ten to the minus twenty-seven seconds.
Switzerland is fine.

**02 · Primordial**

Number two: primordial black holes.
Every normal black hole starts as a dying star. These skipped the star.
A split second after the Big Bang, the universe was a dense, boiling soup.
Slightly denser pockets could have collapsed straight into black holes,
from the mass of an asteroid to thousands of Suns.
Like lumps in gravy that set before the rest of the pot.
Stephen Hawking proposed them in 1971.
An asteroid-mass one would be smaller than an atom.
And any lighter than about five hundred million tons have already evaporated.
A 2024 study found one drifting through the inner solar system could nudge Mars's orbit by about a meter. Enough to measure.
Some physicists think the rest could be part of the universe's missing dark matter.
The catch: we've never found one.
Surveys watch millions of stars, waiting for something invisible to drift in front
and bend their light.
Mostly: nothing. The perfect suspect, zero fingerprints.

**03 · Stellar-mass**

Number three: stellar-mass black holes. The classic.
Take a star at least twenty times heavier than the Sun.
When its fuel runs out, fusion stops pushing back.
The core collapses in under a second,
and the rest explodes as a supernova.
What's left: five to a few dozen Suns, crushed into a ball the size of a city.
Ten Suns, in something about sixty kilometers across.
Cygnus X-1 was the first one we really confirmed,
slurping gas off a giant blue star seven thousand light-years away.
There are probably about a hundred million of them in our galaxy alone.
The closest one we know is about fifteen hundred light-years away.
And the heaviest found in our galaxy, Gaia BH3, packs thirty-three Suns.
The catch: from a distance, they're boring.
Swap the Sun for a black hole of the same mass,
and Earth's orbit doesn't change at all.
You'd freeze in the dark. But you would not get sucked in.

**04 · Intermediate**

Number four: intermediate black holes.
One hundred to one hundred thousand Suns.
They have to exist, because the giant ones didn't appear from nothing.
Smaller black holes had to merge and grow, step by step.
Stellar ones are compact cars. Supermassive ones are cargo ships.
These are the delivery vans that went missing.
The catch: we almost never see them.
Too small to run a galaxy, too quiet to spot, unless they're shredding a star.
In 2019, LIGO caught two black holes merging into one of about a hundred and forty-two Suns.
And in 2024, seven speeding stars in Omega Centauri gave one away: at least eight thousand Suns.

**05 · Supermassive**

Number five: supermassive black holes.
Almost every big galaxy has one in its middle.
Hundreds of thousands to billions of Suns.
Ours is Sagittarius A-star: four point three million Suns,
packed into a space smaller than Mercury's orbit.
In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope photographed it.
Stars near it whip around at about two and a half percent of light speed. Tracking them won the 2020 Nobel Prize.
Shrink the Milky Way to the size of Earth, and that black hole is a grain of sand.
M87's black hole, the first ever photographed, weighs six and a half billion Suns.
And when one feeds hard, it can outshine its entire galaxy.
The catch: bigger is gentler.
Fall into a small one, and the tides stretch you like spaghetti before you reach the edge.
Fall into a supermassive one, and you cross the event horizon without feeling a thing.
Until it's way too late.

**06 · Ultramassive**

Number six: ultramassive black holes.
Ten billion Suns and up.
TON 618 weighs up to sixty-six billion Suns.
Its event horizon reaches up to thirteen hundred astronomical units in radius.
Line up our solar system, out to Neptune, more than forty times side by side,
and you still haven't crossed it.
Its quasar shines like about a hundred and forty trillion Suns,
and the light we see left it about ten billion years ago.
Phoenix A might weigh around a hundred billion Suns. More than all the stars in the Milky Way.
The catch: there's a size limit.
Past roughly fifty billion Suns, depending on spin, its own gravity wrecks the disk that feeds it.
After that, the only way to grow is to crash into another giant.

**07 · Spinning (Kerr)**

Number seven: spinning black holes. Kerr black holes.
A Schwarzschild black hole doesn't spin. That's the simple textbook version.
In 1963, Roy Kerr found the math for one that does.
Stars spin, so almost every real black hole is a Kerr black hole.
One called GRS 1915+105 spins more than a thousand times a second.
The fast ones drag space around them, like a spoon stirring honey.
Inside that zone, the ergosphere, nothing can stand still.
And inside, the singularity isn't a point. It's a ring.
The catch: you can steal its energy.
Throw something into the ergosphere so it splits in two.
One half falls in. The other flies out with more energy than you threw in.
A cosmic battery. Get the angle wrong, and you become part of the battery.

**08 · Charged & white holes**

Number eight: charged black holes and white holes.
Welcome to the math-only wing of the museum.
A Reissner-Nordström black hole carries electric charge, which gives it a second, inner horizon.
A white hole is a black hole in reverse. Nothing gets in. Everything blasts out.
A drain, versus a tap you can never turn off.
The catch: nature hates both.
A charged black hole would pull in opposite charges and neutralize itself almost instantly.
And white holes break the second law of thermodynamics so badly, most physicists call them math only.
Unless, as a few wild ideas suggest, the Big Bang itself was one.

**Outro / end screen**

You don't need a black hole to die in one second. Ten real planets will do it. They're right here.

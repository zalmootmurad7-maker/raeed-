# What If Earth's Oxygen Kept Dropping and Couldn't Stop?

**Format test (2026-11-23).** This copies the format that is working right now for small "what if" channels (Minute Zero 2.4M, What If Lab 574K at 1.4K subs, The Cosmic Witness 630K at 3.1K subs):
- **Escalation instead of one moment.** "...and couldn't stop", with a live counter the viewer waits on.
- **8–10 minutes.**
- **You are inside the scene** (your street, your stairs, your match), not a lecture with diagrams.
- **Thumbnail:** a POV scene + ONE huge number + one small line.

**Kept from our own rules:**
- Hook in the first 5 s with a "you" line.
- A promise ("before this video is over…") paid off at the end.
- A re-hook every 20–30 s.
- One honest assumption up front, then every step anchored to something real.
- Hypotheses said as hypotheses.
- Like CTA, then an end question that leads to the next video.
- Hook delivered [excited], not [serious].
- Few [pause] tags; `tighten.py` removes dead air.

**Length:** ~1,330 words ≈ 8½–9 min at Mark's pace (before tighten).

## Voice script (ElevenLabs v3, voice "Mark")

### PART 1: Hook + setup + first days (record as 1.mp3)

[excited] Every day, Earth's air loses one percent of its oxygen. And it can't stop.
On day six, you strike a match… and nothing happens.
On day fourteen, you're breathing the air from the top of Mount Everest.
And before this video is over, I'll show you the only two places where humans are still breathing normally.

Here's the experiment. Every night at midnight, one percent of the air's oxygen is quietly swapped for nitrogen. Same pressure. Same wind. Same blue sky. Just a little less oxygen.
Right now, the air you're breathing is twenty-one percent oxygen. So we have twenty-one days.
Let's start the clock.

Day one. Twenty percent.
You wake up, and… nothing. No alarm. No breaking news. Your blood is carrying almost exactly as much oxygen as it did yesterday. You would never notice.

Day two. Nineteen percent.
And now, technically, the whole planet is a hazard. In the United States, workplace safety rules call any air below nineteen and a half percent oxygen "oxygen-deficient." Every office, every school, every bedroom on Earth just failed a safety inspection.
But you feel fine. So what's the big deal?

Day four. Seventeen percent.
Your body now thinks you moved to Denver, the Mile High City. On the stairs, you breathe a little harder. Your heart beats a little faster. Annoying, but millions of people live like this every day.
But something else is starting to struggle. And it isn't you.

### PART 2: Fire, altitude, the long climb (record as 2.mp3)

Day five. Sixteen percent.
Candles start to flicker and die. The flame on your gas stove shrinks, turns weak, and sputters.

Day six. Fifteen percent.
You strike a match. It flares for a moment… and dies.
Here's why. A flame doesn't care how thin the air is. It cares how much of the mix is oxygen. The rest is nitrogen, and nitrogen steals the flame's heat. Below about fifteen percent, most ordinary fires can't even get started.
And we've seen this happen. Some data centers and archives keep their air at about fifteen percent oxygen on purpose, so a fire can never start. And people still walk in and work there.
Which means something strange. You would outlive fire.
No campfires. No candles. No burning trash. Car engines squeeze the air before they burn it, so they'd probably keep running, just weaker, like driving high in the mountains.
But you're about to go much higher than any mountain road.

Day eight. Thirteen percent.
Your body now thinks you're almost four thousand meters up. That's higher than La Paz in Bolivia, where more than two million people live every day. But they grew up there. You didn't.
So you get altitude sickness. A pounding headache. Nausea. You can't sleep, and when you do, you wake up gasping.
Here's the real problem. Mountaineers have a rule: once you're high up, don't climb more than about five hundred meters a day, or your body can't keep up. In this experiment, you're climbing more than that every single day. And the steps keep getting bigger. Without moving an inch.

Day ten. Eleven percent.
You're now breathing the air of La Rinconada in Peru, the highest town on Earth, five thousand one hundred meters up.
This is where the safety charts get dark. Your lips turn blue. Your judgment slips. You make mistakes you'd never believe you could make.
Hospitals move every patient they can onto oxygen tanks. But the factories that make medical oxygen pull it out of the same air that's running out.

Day eleven. Ten percent.
Higher than Everest Base Camp. Climbers spend days there just to get used to the air, and they have doctors, tents, and a way down.
There is no way down now. People start fainting in the street.

### PART 3: Death zone, oceans, the twist, the end (record as 3.mp3)

Day thirteen. Eight percent.
You've reached the edge of what climbers call the death zone. Up here, the human body is slowly dying, no matter how fit you are.

Day fourteen. Seven percent.
The summit of Mount Everest.
And humans have breathed this. In 1978, Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler reached the top of Everest without bottled oxygen. But they'd spent weeks getting ready, and they stayed on the summit for minutes, not days.
Most people would collapse here. And they wouldn't get back up.

Meanwhile, the oceans. Fish breathe oxygen dissolved in water, and the surface of the ocean slowly follows the air above it.
At six percent, surface water would hold only about two milligrams of oxygen per liter. That's the exact level scientists use to define a "dead zone," like the one that appears in the Gulf of Mexico every summer, where fish either flee or die.
Now imagine that dead zone… everywhere.

Day sixteen. Five percent.
On the safety charts, air like this has one word next to it. Coma. A person dropped into it suddenly passes out in under a minute.
So who's still breathing?

Remember my promise. Here are the only two places where humans are still breathing normally.
One: the International Space Station. Astronauts don't bring all their air from Earth. They make fresh oxygen by splitting water with electricity.
Two: nuclear submarines, deep underwater, doing the exact same thing, splitting water into oxygen and hydrogen.
So the last humans breathing normally would be a few hundred sailors under the sea… and a handful of astronauts above it. Watching the planet go quiet.

Day twenty-one. Zero.
And here's the strangest part. Earth has been here before.
For its first two billion years, our planet had almost no oxygen in the air at all. Life was microbes that didn't need it. And some of them are still here today, in deep mud, in hot springs, inside rocks. They'd barely notice.
On day twenty-one, Earth wouldn't be dead. It would just be… young again.

If this blew your mind, hit like.
And if you're wondering what happens when oxygen goes the other way… Earth has been there too. About three hundred million years ago, oxygen climbed past thirty percent, and dragonflies flew with wings seventy centimeters wide.
So what happens if it keeps rising… and can't stop?
That's the next experiment.

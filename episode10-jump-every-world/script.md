    Pluto → Part 3 already opens on a teaser.

## Voice script (3 parts)

### Part 1
```text
[curious] Jump as hard as you can right now, and your feet will clear the ground by about half a meter. [matter-of-fact] On Mars, that exact same jump launches you one point three meters—high enough to leap over a sports car. On the Moon, you soar three meters straight up. On Jupiter, gravity slams you back down after a pathetic twenty centimeters. [pause] [serious] And if you try that exact same jump on the wrong world in our solar system... you will literally never touch the ground again.

[curious] So how high can human legs actually take you across the solar system? And where is the exact borderline between a fun low-gravity bounce... and accidentally launching yourself into orbit around the Sun?

[matter-of-fact] Here are the rules: same person, same legs, same jump everywhere, a push-off at three point one three meters per second. On Earth, that gets you half a meter up, and you're back on the ground in just over half a second.
[deadpan] Your legs are a spring. Now let's take that spring to the heaviest neighborhood in the solar system.

[serious] First stop: Jupiter. Now, Jupiter doesn't have a solid surface, so let's bolt a floating titanium platform right at its one-bar cloud layer, where atmospheric pressure matches Earth at sea level. Here, gravity is nearly two and a half times stronger than home. Step onto the platform, and a seventy-kilogram human instantly feels like they weigh a hundred and seventy-seven kilograms. [strained] Bend your knees, fire every muscle fiber you have, and... [deadpan] thud. You clear just zero point two meters—barely the height of a soda can—and slam back down in a quarter of a second.
[curious] So surely the other giant planets crush you too... right? Not quite. One of them actually lets you jump higher than you do on Earth.

[curious] On windy Neptune, gravity is only twelve percent stronger than Earth's, so you still manage a respectable forty-four-centimeter hop before winds faster than any hurricane on Earth blow you sideways.
[curious] And the next giant breaks the rule completely.

[amazed] On Uranus, something genuinely weird happens. Uranus is fourteen and a half times more massive than Earth. Yet if you jump from a platform in its upper clouds, you reach zero point five six meters—actually jumping six centimeters higher than you do on Earth. [matter-of-fact] Why? Because Uranus is basically a giant, low-density slushball of water, ammonia, and methane ice. Its volume is so puffed up that the cloud tops sit twenty-five thousand kilometers away from its center, making gravity at the top eleven percent weaker than Earth's.

[deadpan] Saturn plays the exact same trick, letting you jump fifty-five centimeters—the exact same height you'd get on Venus. Though on Venus, jumping through carbon dioxide air ninety-two times denser than Earth's feels less like track-and-field, and more like trying to jump at the bottom of a boiling swimming pool.
[excited] But all of that is just warming up. Because on the next worlds... you start to fly.
```

### Part 2
```text
[excited] Things get much more interesting when we head to the smaller rocky worlds: Mars and Mercury.

[matter-of-fact] On Mars, surface gravity drops to just thirty-eight percent of Earth's. Fire your legs at that same three point one three meters per second, and you float upward to one point three two meters, hanging in the air for nearly one point seven seconds. [amused] If the NBA ever builds a stadium inside a pressurized Martian crater, every single person in the bleachers will be able to do a standing slam dunk on a regulation three-meter hoop without even taking a running start.
[curious] Now here's a puzzle. The next planet has only half the mass of Mars... and it gives you the exact same jump.

[curious] Hop over to Mercury, and your tape measure reads... one point three two meters. Again. [surprised] Which makes zero sense at first glance, because Mars has nearly twice the mass of Mercury. [matter-of-fact] The secret is hidden underground. While Mars is made mostly of lighter silicate rock, Mercury is basically a giant iron cannonball: its metal core fills about eighty-five percent of the planet's radius, wrapped in a thin rocky crust. That extreme density packs Mercury's smaller mass much closer to your boots, giving Mars and Mercury almost exactly the same surface gravity.
[curious] Next stop is a world you've seen almost every night of your life. And it more than doubles your jump.

[excited] Ready to clear a whole room? Let's head to Earth's Moon. With just one-sixth of Earth's gravity, your half-meter Earth hop turns into a three-meter vertical launch, with almost four full seconds of hang time. [curious] Now, if you've watched footage of the Apollo astronauts, you might be thinking: wait a second, they only managed small bunny hops. [matter-of-fact] True—because they were wearing an eighty-one-kilogram life-support suit pressurized like a rigid truck tire that wouldn't let them bend their knees. Take off the suit inside an indoor lunar gym, and a normal jump puts your head through the first-floor ceiling.
[curious] But one moon in the solar system is even better. There, you don't just jump... you fly.

[amazed] Go out to Saturn's largest moon, Titan, and gravity drops to fourteen percent of Earth's. Here, one jump sends you three point six meters into the orange haze—high enough to leap clean over Robert Wadlow, the tallest human in recorded history, with nearly a meter to spare. [excited] Better yet, Titan's air is thick: one and a half times Earth's pressure, and about four times as dense. With gravity that weak and air that thick, physicists have calculated that if you strap a pair of wings to your arms and jump, you don't even have to come down—you can literally flap your arms and fly under your own muscle power.
[curious] And from here on, jumps stop being measured in meters... and start being measured in buildings.

[matter-of-fact] Keep heading out into the deep freeze of the Kuiper Belt, and the numbers start looking like a glitch in the simulation. On the dwarf planet Eris, a single jump carries you six meters up—high enough to look down on a full-grown giraffe. And on Pluto, where gravity is barely six percent of Earth's, your half-meter jump explodes into a seven-point-nine-meter soar. [deadpan] You would clear the roof of a two-story suburban house and spend ten full seconds floating in complete silence before your boots touched the nitrogen ice again.
```

### Part 3
```text
[serious] Ten seconds of hang time sounds extreme. But we haven't visited the small moons and comets yet—where jumping stops being a sport, and starts becoming an orbital mechanics problem.

[curious] Take Phobos, the larger of Mars's two potato-shaped moons. Phobos is only twenty-two kilometers across, and its gravity is seventeen hundred times weaker than Earth's. Stand on Phobos and jump with your standard three point one three meters per second push-off. [amazed] You won't just clear a house. You will rise about nine hundred meters into the black sky—higher than the tip of the Burj Khalifa, the tallest skyscraper on Earth. [deadpan] And because gravity up there is so weak, your trip up and back down takes about twenty minutes. You could jump, listen to four songs, eat a sandwich, and still be waiting to land.
[curious] And Mars has a second moon, even smaller... where a little push is all it takes to leave forever.

[serious] Hop over to Mars's smaller moon, Deimos. Here, a standing jump sends you almost three kilometers high, and you won't come back down for over an hour. [excited] And notice how dangerously close we're getting to the ultimate speed limit: escape velocity. On Earth, you'd have to jump at eleven point two kilometers per second—Mach thirty-three—to break free of gravity forever. On Deimos, escape velocity is just five point six meters per second. [deadpan] A standing jump won't quite reach it. And you can't take a run-up either: with gravity this weak, your very first step would launch you off the ground. So grab a pole bolted into the rock and fling yourself off it, like a swimmer pushing off a wall. Hit five point six meters per second... and you never come back. You become a brand-new, very tiny moon of Mars.
[curious] But on one world in the solar system, you don't need a pole. Or a run-up. Just a normal jump.

[amazed] Welcome to Comet sixty-seven P, the four-kilometer icy world visited by the Rosetta spacecraft. Its gravity is so whisper-faint that its escape velocity is just one meter per second—a slow walking pace. [matter-of-fact] And we've watched how weak that gravity is. In twenty fourteen, the Philae lander touched down on this comet, bounced off its surface, and drifted for almost two hours before it finally came back down. Philae was moving at less than half a meter per second. [deadpan] Bend your knees on Comet sixty-seven P and do a normal half-meter Earth jump at three point one three meters per second, and you leave at more than triple its escape speed. No rockets. No countdown. Just one enthusiastic hop, and congratulations—you are officially an independent asteroid orbiting the Sun.

[curious] So if you had a pressurized dome on any world in the solar system, which gravity level are you picking: slam-dunking on Mars, flying with wings on Titan, or taking a twenty-minute skyscraper jump on Phobos? Drop your pick in the comments, and subscribe to Ink and Atoms before our stickman drifts past Jupiter.
```

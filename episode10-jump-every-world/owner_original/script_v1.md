### Part 1: The Hook, The Physics & The Heavyweights (`00:00 – 02:15`)

```text
[curious] Jump as hard as you can right now, and your feet will clear the ground by about half a meter. [matter-of-fact] On Mars, that exact same jump launches you one point three meters—high enough to leap over a sports car. On the Moon, you soar three meters straight up. On Jupiter, gravity slams you back down after a pathetic twenty centimeters. [pause] [serious] And if you try that exact same jump on the wrong moon in our solar system... you will literally never touch the ground again.

[curious] So how high can human legs actually take you across the solar system? And where is the exact borderline between a fun low-gravity bounce... and accidentally launching yourself into orbit around the Sun?

[matter-of-fact] To make this a fair contest, we have to standardize the human. An athletic standing vertical jump on Earth gets your center of mass up by zero point five meters—about twenty inches. To reach that height against Earth's gravity of nine point eight meters per second squared, your legs have to push off the ground at exactly three point one three meters per second, or about seven miles per hour. And the whole trip—up and down—is over in just zero point six four seconds.

[deadpan] Think of your legs as a biological spring loaded with three point one three meters per second of velocity. Now let's take that spring to the heaviest neighborhood in the solar system.

[serious] First stop: Jupiter. Now, Jupiter doesn't have a solid surface, so let's bolt a floating titanium platform right at its one-bar cloud layer, where atmospheric pressure matches Earth at sea level. Here, gravity is nearly two and a half times stronger than home. Step onto the platform, and a seventy-kilogram human instantly feels like they weigh a hundred and seventy-seven kilograms. [strained] Bend your knees, fire every muscle fiber you have, and... [deadpan] thud. You clear just zero point two meters—barely the height of a soda can—and slam back down in a quarter of a second. 

[curious] What about the other giant planets? You might expect Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune to crush your knees just like Jupiter. [surprised] Nope. On windy Neptune, gravity is only twelve percent stronger than Earth's, so you still manage a respectable forty-four-centimeter hop before supersonic methane winds blow you sideways. 

[amazed] And on Uranus, something genuinely weird happens. Uranus is fourteen and a half times more massive than Earth. Yet if you jump from a platform in its upper clouds, you reach zero point five six meters—actually jumping six centimeters higher than you do on Earth. [matter-of-fact] Why? Because Uranus is basically a giant, low-density slushball of water, ammonia, and methane ice. Its volume is so puffed up that the cloud tops sit twenty-five thousand kilometers away from its heavy core, making gravity at the top eleven percent weaker than Earth's.

[deadpan] Saturn plays the exact same trick, letting you jump fifty-five centimeters—the exact same height you'd get on Venus. Though on Venus, jumping through carbon dioxide air ninety-two times denser than Earth's feels less like track-and-field, and more like trying to jump at the bottom of a boiling swimming pool.
```

### Part 2: The Dunk Contest & The Slow-Motion Worlds (`02:15 – 04:55`)

```text
[excited] Things get much more interesting when we head to the smaller rocky worlds: Mars and Mercury. 

[matter-of-fact] On Mars, surface gravity drops to just thirty-eight percent of Earth's. Fire your legs at that same three point one three meters per second, and you float upward to one point three two meters—over four feet of pure vertical clearance—hanging in the air for nearly one point seven seconds. [amused] If the NBA ever builds a stadium inside a pressurized Martian crater, every single person in the bleachers will be able to do a standing slam dunk on a regulation ten-foot hoop without even taking a running start.

[curious] Now hop over to Mercury, and your tape measure reads... one point three two meters. Again. [surprised] Which makes zero sense at first glance, because Mars has nearly twice the mass of Mercury. [matter-of-fact] The secret is hidden underground. While Mars is made mostly of lighter silicate rock, Mercury is basically a giant cannonball of solid iron wrapped in a thin rocky crust. That extreme density packs Mercury's smaller mass much closer to your boots, giving Mars and Mercury the exact same surface gravity down to the second decimal place.

[excited] Ready to clear a whole room? Let's head to Earth's Moon. With just one-sixth of Earth's gravity, your half-meter Earth hop turns into a three-meter vertical launch. That's nearly ten feet straight up, with almost four full seconds of hang time. [curious] Now, if you've watched footage of the Apollo astronauts, you might be thinking: wait a second, Neil Armstrong only hopped about half a meter off the lunar dirt. [matter-of-fact] True—because he was wearing an eighty-one-kilogram life-support suit pressurized like a rigid truck tire that wouldn't let him bend his knees. Take off the suit inside an indoor lunar gym, and a normal jump puts your head through the first-floor ceiling.

[amazed] Go even further out to Saturn's largest moon, Titan, and gravity drops to fourteen percent of Earth's. Here, one jump sends you three point six meters into the orange haze—high enough to leap clean over Robert Wadlow, the tallest human in recorded history, with nearly a meter to spare. [excited] Better yet, Titan's nitrogen atmosphere is fifty percent denser than Earth's. With gravity that weak and air that thick, physicists have calculated that if you strap a pair of synthetic wings to your arms and jump, you don't even have to come down—you can literally flap your arms and fly under your own muscle power.

[matter-of-fact] Keep heading out into the deep freeze of the Kuiper Belt, and the numbers start looking like a glitch in the simulation. On the dwarf planet Eris, a single jump carries you five and a half meters up—letting you look a full-grown giraffe dead in the eye. And on Pluto, where gravity is barely six percent of Earth's, your half-meter jump explodes into a seven-point-nine-meter soar. [deadpan] You would clear the roof of a two-story suburban house and spend ten full seconds floating in complete silence before your boots touched the nitrogen ice again.
```

### Part 3: The Escape Velocity Trap (`04:55 – 06:35`)

```text
[serious] Ten seconds of hang time sounds extreme. But we haven't visited the small moons and asteroids yet—where jumping stops being a sport, and starts becoming an orbital mechanics problem.

[curious] Take Phobos, the larger of Mars's two potato-shaped moons. Phobos is only twenty-two kilometers across, and its gravity is seventeen hundred times weaker than Earth's. Stand on Phobos and jump with your standard three point one three meters per second push-off. [amazed] You won't just clear a house. You will rise eight hundred and sixty meters into the black sky—thirty-two meters higher than the tip of the Burj Khalifa, the tallest skyscraper on Earth. [deadpan] And because gravity is pulling you back at just half a centimeter per second squared, your trip up and back down will take eighteen minutes. You could jump, listen to four songs, eat a sandwich, and still be waiting to land.

[serious] Now hop over to Mars's smaller moon, Deimos. Here, a standing jump sends you one point six kilometers high for thirty-five minutes. [excited] And notice how dangerously close we're getting to the ultimate speed limit: escape velocity. On Earth, you'd have to jump at eleven point two kilometers per second—Mach thirty-three—to break free of gravity forever. On Deimos, escape velocity is just five point six meters per second, or twelve miles per hour. [deadpan] A standing jump won't quite reach it, but if you build a short four-step staircase on Deimos and sprint off the top step, you will exceed escape velocity and launch yourself straight into solar orbit.

[amazed] Want to do it from a standing start? Welcome to Comet sixty-seven P, the four-kilometer icy world visited by the Rosetta spacecraft. Its gravity is so whisper-faint that its escape velocity is just one meter per second—a casual walking pace.
```
